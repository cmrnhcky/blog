     GREATER THAN — the shape

     The title IS the argument. "A $400 suit that fits > a $2,000 suit
     that doesn't." Write the title first; if it doesn't work as a
     standalone line, the piece won't either.

     1. State both sides fairly in two sentences. If the losing side
        is a strawman, nobody believes the verdict.
     2. Give the losing side its best argument. Genuinely its best.
     3. Then the turn — the thing the losing argument doesn't account for.
     4. Verdict. Unhedged. No "of course, it depends."

     THE RULE: the piece must be falsifiable. Someone who disagrees
     should be able to point at the exact sentence they'd fight.
     "Both have their place" is a failure, not a conclusion.

     LENGTH: 300-500 words. This form runs slightly longer than an
     endorsement because it has to be fair to two things.

     comments, search for "▶".

▶ THE SETUP, ONE PARAGRAPH. Both things, named, neutrally. No verdict yet. The reader should not be able to tell which way you are going from the first paragraph — that is what makes them read the second.

▶ NOW ARGUE THE SIDE YOU ARE ABOUT TO BEAT. This is the paragraph most people skip, and skipping it is why most "this vs that" writing is worthless. Give it the strongest version. If you cannot write a convincing case for the loser, you have not understood the question well enough to answer it.

▶ THE TURN. One sentence, and it should feel like a door opening. "All of that is true, and none of it survives contact with ▶ THE THING IT IGNORES."

▶ THE CASE. Two or three paragraphs. Concrete, not abstract — a number, a scene, a specific instance beats a principle every time. If you find yourself writing "quality matters," replace it with the thing that made you believe quality matters.

▶ THE VERDICT. Restate the title as a flat declarative. Do not soften it. Do not add "for most people." The whole form is a promise that you will actually pick one.
