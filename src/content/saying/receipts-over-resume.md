<span class="lede">Nobody believes</span> a word you write about yourself, and they are right not to.

The résumé is a document in which the subject is also the author, the editor and the fact-checker. Everybody reading one knows this. So the genre has collapsed into a compliance exercise — a thing you produce because it is required, scanned for six seconds by somebody hunting for a reason to stop.

Meanwhile, for ten years, strangers have been keeping a second file on you. Every stay, every trip, every sale, every week spent in somebody's house looking after their dog. A person with money at risk sat down afterward and recorded whether you did what you said you would do. That file is longer than your résumé, more specific than your portfolio, and written by people who had no reason to be kind.

You cannot show it to anyone.

The résumé deserves a proper defence, because it does something nothing else does. It is the only artifact that can describe what you are *aiming at* rather than what you have already finished. It is the only place a career reads as a line instead of a pile. And it has relevance on its side, which is not a small thing: a five-star rating for returning a car with a full tank tells a prospective client precisely nothing about whether you can build them a website. Nobody hires a marketer because he is tidy. The portfolio is at least about the work, and the work is what is being bought.

**Every one of those platforms made your reputation non-portable on purpose, because portability is the only thing that would ever let you leave.**

That is not an oversight in the product. It is the product. The rating is the switching cost. A host with four hundred trips and a 4.98 is not staying because the software is good; he is staying because the four hundred trips do not come with him. You spent a decade paying rent into a building you will never own, and the rent was paid in good behaviour.

The receipts also survive a harder test than a portfolio does. A portfolio is self-selected — you show three projects out of eleven and quietly decline to mention the other eight. A review is compulsory and unedited. It is written by the counterparty, immediately, whether it went well or not. And it measures the thing a buyer is actually frightened of. Not *is he any good* — everyone assumes you are competent, or they would not have called. The fear is smaller and more humiliating than that: will he answer the phone in week three. Will he say the thing I do not want to hear. Anybody who has hired a freelancer has been burned by someone talented.

So here is the honest limit, stated before somebody else states it for me: a decade of marketplace reviews proves you are a good counterparty. It does not prove you are a good marketer. Those are different claims and a reader can tell them apart instantly. Which is why the receipts belong inside an argument and never on a wall. A page headed *My Reviews* is a man showing you his medals. A linked, checkable fact inside a piece you actually wrote is evidence.

The rule that follows is simple enough to hold to. If it cannot be clicked, it does not count. A screenshot of a review count is worth nothing — everybody knows it takes four minutes to fake one, and the person you are trying to impress is the one person who has thought about that. Link to the live profile or leave it out.

The résumé is a claim. The receipt is a witness.
