<span class="lede">The alarm goes</span> at four fifty and there is no version of that which feels good. ▶ Open in the middle of something. A time, a place, a physical detail. Not a thesis — the thesis can wait two paragraphs.

▶ Second paragraph carries the scene a little further. Resist explaining yourself yet.

<aside class="sidenote">
  <b>The rule</b>
  ▶ A sidenote is for the thing that is true but would break the paragraph's stride: a caveat, a number, a piece of gear, a correction. One or two sentences. If it needs three, it is a paragraph.
</aside>

▶ The paragraph the sidenote is commenting on. Notice that it has to stand entirely on its own — a reader on a phone meets the note before this paragraph, and a reader on a laptop meets it alongside.

<div class="dinkus" role="separator" aria-hidden="true"><span></span></div>

▶ The dinkus is a section break. Use it where a piece genuinely turns — from scene to argument, from then to now. Two in an article is plenty. Three means it wants to be two articles.

▶ Numbers do a specific job in a fitness piece: they stop it being inspirational. Put the real ones in, including the ones that are not impressive.

<aside class="sidenote">
  <b>▶ Second note</b>
  ▶ Two sidenotes in a piece is the ceiling. They stack down the right margin and start to compete with the column for attention.
</aside>

▶ Step back exactly once, near the end, and say what this is actually about. One paragraph. Then stop — an essay that explains its own moral twice has not trusted the first time.

▶ Last line. Short. It is the only sentence most people will remember.
