<span class="lede">You do not need</span> a scraper. Every platform on this list has a button that hands you the whole record, because a law in Europe and another in California made it cheaper to build the button than to argue about it. Scraping your own profile from behind a login breaks most of their terms and breaks again the next time they ship a redesign. The file is free. Ask for the file.

The ranking is by one rule, and it is the same rule that governs whether any of this is worth doing: **how much of it can a stranger verify without taking your word for it.** A public URL somebody can open on their phone beats a complete data export nobody but you will ever see. A number inside an app is worth nothing at all. So the platforms that let you *link* outrank the platforms that only let you *download*, however generous the download.

<ol class="ranked">

  <li>
    <h3>eBay</h3>
    <p class="ranked__meta">Public profile · CSV export · the only one that gives you both</p>
    <p>Your feedback profile sits at a permanent public address — ebay.com/fdbk/feedback_profile/ plus your username — and anyone can read it without an account, an app, or your permission. Seller Hub → Reports → Downloads pulls the underlying record as CSV. Nothing else here does both halves cleanly. Worth knowing: the score itself is not transferable, which sellers rediscover every time they move to Shopify. The URL is the asset. The number is just what is printed on it.</p>
  </li>

  <li>
    <h3>Turo</h3>
    <p class="ranked__meta">Public profile · no export worth the name</p>
    <p>Host profiles are public at turo.com/drivers/ plus your numeric id — trip count, reviews, all of it legible to anyone holding the link. That link is the entire asset, so link it and screenshot nothing. Two catches: guest-side history is not public, so this only pays if you hosted, and there is no clean export, so the profile page is both the proof and the only copy.</p>
  </li>

  <li>
    <h3>Etsy</h3>
    <p class="ranked__meta">Public shop reviews · full self-serve export</p>
    <p>Reviews live on the public shop page permanently, no login. The export is Account settings → Privacy → Download Data → Request your data. The catch is closure: a closed shop can take its page with it, so pull the export <em>before</em> you shut anything down, not after you think of it.</p>
  </li>

  <li>
    <h3>Airbnb</h3>
    <p class="ranked__meta">Best export on the list · only half of it public</p>
    <p>Account → Privacy &amp; sharing → Request your personal data, and it offers JSON rather than a PDF you cannot query — take the JSON. It arrives as a zip and the download link expires, so collect it the day the email lands. It ranks here rather than first because the public half is one-sided: a host profile carries its reviews in the open, a guest's history is not listable anywhere, and most people were guests.</p>
  </li>

  <li>
    <h3>Poshmark</h3>
    <p class="ranked__meta">Public closet · nothing to download</p>
    <p>The closet is public and carries ratings and love notes, which is more than half this list manages. There is no export worth the name, so treat the closet page the way you treat the Turo profile — it is the copy of record. Link it and leave it alone.</p>
  </li>

  <li>
    <h3>Uber</h3>
    <p class="ranked__meta">Comprehensive export · nothing public at all</p>
    <p>myprivacy.uber.com/privacy/exploreyourdata → Download your data. Allow up to thirty days, which is worth starting now rather than the week you need it. The file is thorough and the public surface is zero: your rating is a number inside an app, visible to you, unverifiable by anybody. Good for knowing your own numbers. Useless as a link, which is what the ranking is measuring.</p>
  </li>

  <li>
    <h3>TrustedHousesitters</h3>
    <p class="ranked__meta">The strongest reviews here · behind a login</p>
    <p>Somebody left you alone in their house with their dog and then wrote down how it went. That is the most load-bearing thing a stranger will ever say about you, and sitter profiles largely sit behind a login where nobody you are trying to convince will ever reach it. So this is the one you convert rather than link — go and ask a named human for a sentence, because the URL will not carry it for you.</p>
  </li>

  <li>
    <h3>Lyft</h3>
    <p class="ranked__meta">No public profile · no self-serve export</p>
    <p>Ride history exports from the Ride History tab in the app, and anything broader is a request through the privacy page rather than a button you press. No public profile either. Last for the obvious reason: it fails both halves of the test.</p>
  </li>

</ol>

<div class="pq">The export is not the proof. The export is where you find the people who can give you the proof.</div>

That is the move most people miss, and it is worth more than everything above it. A star rating is the weakest thing in the file — it is a number a stranger chose from a list of five, and it converts to nothing you can quote. What the export actually contains is **names and dates**: who you dealt with, when, and how it went. Three of those people will write you a sentence if you ask, and one sentence from a named human outranks any rating on any platform, because it is the only version of this that survives somebody deciding to check.

So the order is: pull the file, read it for the names, ask the three, and link the two or three profiles that a stranger can open cold. Everything else is a screenshot, and a screenshot is worth nothing.

<p class="endnote">Paths verified September 2026; they move, and the export buttons exist because of the GDPR and California's privacy acts rather than any generosity. Florida's own Digital Bill of Rights reaches only companies above a billion in global revenue that also make half their money from advertising, run an app store, or sell a smart speaker — which excludes most of this list. None of the platforms geo-gated the button anyway. Use it while that stays true.</p>
